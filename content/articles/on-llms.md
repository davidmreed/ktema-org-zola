Software engineering is a form of translation, and an intellectual practice.

We seek to understand a human problem of some sort. We translate that problem, full of nuance and contingency
and divergence, into ideas within the tightly-bounded framework of what a computer can do. In doing so we apply
our knowledge of people, of the problem on which we operate, and the principles of the engineering framework.
We take joy in that process. We exercise our minds, working to a greater or lesser extent both our empathic
faculties and those portions of ourselves that delight in rules and strictures. When we're finished, we hope,
we make someone's life a little nicer (perhaps our own). Along the way, we relish the struggle and the
moments of discovery and the overcomings of our failures.

What's beautiful about software engineering is that it is impure. We work in logic, yes, in rigorously
constructed and constrained languages and capabilities, but that logic runs on physical, fallible machines.
Those machines are made by and for humans. Even the most abstract of algorithmic work ultimately has some
human referent and some engagement with translation. Engineering sits on cusps that are pleasing to cross
and re-cross and challenge.

"Generative AI" vitiates everything I value about software engineering.

Prompting an LLM to produce plausible code might look like a translation exercise from a certain angle.
But it's really not. "Translate" comes from the Latin _translatio_: to carry across. With an LLM, there's
no across.

A prompt is not so much pressing a button on a black box in the hopes of obtaining
a desired output as it is pressing a hundred buttons in some order, where neither the selection of buttons
nor their ordering obeys any real principles but the satisfaction of the stochastic demon inside the box.

AI also sits on cusps, but very different ones. It sits on the cusp between meaning and non-meaning.
More precisely, it offers us the _appearance_ of meaning without the substance.

It is neither artificial, nor intelligence.
It's not at all intelligent: Large Language Models do not understand concepts; they write plausible phrases
and texts. And on several levels, it is very, very human.

LLMs are human in that they're distilled from millions of works of human authorship. They are also human in
that they exist to satisfy human goals and desires, and that they play to our foibles.

When I was eight or nine years old, my grandmother gave me a collection of manuals and let me explore her computers.
These machines were at the time about as old as I was: a Texas Instruments TI/99 4A and a 1983 Compaq PC clone.
I remember vividly the delight I took when I ran the BASIC code

```basic
10 PRINT "DAVID"
20 GOTO 10
```

and watched my name scroll endlessly on the console.

What is that delight in writing code, which I still feel today, about?

It's certainly not about the output. (I wasn't burdened with the need to type my own name indefinitely,
and forced to turn to automation!) It's not about having the code, either. (Those lines might be on
a 5 1/4" floppy somewhere, but it makes no difference).

Is it about the power? Seeing one's will on the screen? Perhaps a bit, but even for a 9-year-old, that glee
is transitory. It's certainly not enough to drive me for thirty years to write code.

No, it's about solving the problem, and the mental tools that solving the problem gives you.

Imagine you're assembling a machine. The pieces come in a box, and you have only loose instructions
on how to fit them together. You hold them in your hands and turn them over, trying different orientations
and pairings of components. Again and again it fails, you grit your teeth, until at just a moment - hah! -
they snap together, both in your mind and in your hands. You have a working machine, and you understand
the machine. If you had another machine, a different one, that worked on the same principles, you could
put it together too.

That's what it feels like to me when I solve a problem in code. That sense of things snapping into place.
And while I have many tools in my mental toolbox beyond `20 GOTO 10`, I think I can say that there's
a continuous line of growth since I was 9.

Here's a few sentences of Thucydides, which I grabbed from the Funeral Oration of Pericles (book II, chapter 41, sections 1-3).
It's a famously lovely and difficult speech. In Greek, this section runs

> ξυνελών τε λέγω τήν τε πᾶσαν πόλιν τῆς Ἑλλάδος παίδευσιν εἶναι καὶ καθ᾽ ἕκαστον δοκεῖν ἄν μοι τὸν αὐτὸν ἄνδρα παρ᾽ ἡμῶν ἐπὶ πλεῖστ᾽ ἂν εἴδη καὶ μετὰ χαρίτων μάλιστ᾽ ἂν εὐτραπέλως τὸ σῶμα αὔταρκες παρέχεσθαι. [2] καὶ ὡς οὐ λόγων ἐν τῷ παρόντι κόμπος τάδε μᾶλλον ἢ ἔργων ἐστὶν ἀλήθεια, αὐτὴ ἡ δύναμις τῆς πόλεως, ἣν ἀπὸ τῶνδε τῶν τρόπων ἐκτησάμεθα, σημαίνει. [3] μόνη γὰρ τῶν νῦν ἀκοῆς κρείσσων ἐς πεῖραν ἔρχεται, καὶ μόνη οὔτε τῷ πολεμίῳ ἐπελθόντι ἀγανάκτησιν ἔχει ὑφ᾽ οἵων κακοπαθεῖ οὔτε τῷ ὑπηκόῳ κατάμεμψιν ὡς οὐχ ὑπ᾽ ἀξίων ἄρχεται.

A sort of word-for-word equivalence might run something like this.

> Drawing [things] together, I say that the entire city is the education of Hellas, and it seems to me that each man among us is able to provide for himself in the widest spate of circumstances with grace and flexibility. And that I do not speak thus in the present circumstance as an affectation but as the truth of affairs, the power of the states itself, which we have acquired through these
> habits, indicates.
>

Thucydides' prose style, particularly in the speeches, is - I might say not florid but eccentric.
His sentences are long and elliptical and unbalanced; he often seems to go out of his way to
break the elegant rhetorical forms that a writer like Lysias might employ.

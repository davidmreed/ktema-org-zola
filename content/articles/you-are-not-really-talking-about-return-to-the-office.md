Companies and leaders that believe they need in-office work to succeed usually have critical weaknesses that have little or nothing to do with offices or with #RemoteWork.

Take this article from Fortune for example. I'm not going to respond to the headline assertion from Sam Altman, which doesn't appear to be backed by any data. Look at the other claims in the piece.

Quoting VC Keith Rabois, we hear:

> Younger workers [...] “learn by osmosis” in a way that requires in-person interaction, and supervisors discover hidden talent by watching them.

I think there’s another way to frame this. What if I flipped the claim around, and said to you instead:

> Many companies struggle to train junior staff members effectively. Instead of preparing high quality onboarding operations, role-based documentation, and other critical learning aids, they rely on informal office co-location — which exposes their junior employees to the vagaries of whoever might be sitting next to them.

Neither of these claims actually has much to do with in-office or remote work. But when we think about the challenge from the perspective of a distributed organization, we find that an argument for in-office work is actually hiding a business weakness. Shouldn’t we train and onboard our employees in ways that are measureable, that are captureable, that are reportable? Why would we go backwards to a world where every onboarding is a one-off interaction, whose success or failure we cannot measure and which is driven as much by who sits next to whom as by rigorous understanding of the business?

Again, we hear from James Clarke (a man who has recently been in the news for some fairly repugnant conversations with his staff!) that he

> ... worries employees might be secretly working multiple jobs while working remotely. He also worries some might be freeing up time by using A.I. tools like OpenAI’s ChatGPT and GPT-4—time that his company isn’t utilizing.

What a colossal admission that Mr. Clarke does not know how to measure the performance of his team, or that his managers cannot tell who is bringing the full measure of their capacity to work! Why shouldn’t we say, instead,

> Highly successful companies in 2023 measure workers based on output, not the number of hours worked. And, as AI technology rapidly matures, companies must have a vision for harnessing AI-driven productivity gains by freeing their workers from boilerplate tasks. The companies that win in 2024 will be those who find ways to use AI to unleash their employees’ creative time, delight their customers, and create more value than ever before.

Flip the script around again. It's not about remote work or in-office work per se. But the argument for in-office work is actually hiding key business weaknesses, and failing to grasp new opportunities to create value. You don’t need to drive people back into the office so that they won’t use AI — you need to create contexts for them to use AI well, and in ways that multiply their impact on the bottom line!

And lastly, from Arvind Krishna of IBM:

> [Krishna] also said while you might be just as productive working from home, “your career does suffer” if you work remotely.

Performance measurement again! Isn’t Krishna telling us here that IBM workers’ career ladders aren’t based on objective metrics, on productivity, on impact — but rather on social connections? That’s not something we should hold up as a model. Quite the opposite. We want to build a career ladder that rewards performance, vision, and impact, not schmoozing. We want to be able to show, with hard data, that we’re meeting our equity goals and promoting top performers.Needless to say, I find Altman’s contention unpersuasive, and not backed by any hard data. But I also want to highlight how many of the arguments against remote work actually have little to do with remote work, and highlight how ineffective aspects of the speakers’ businesses are.

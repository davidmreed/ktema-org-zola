Companies and leaders that believe they need in-office work to succeed usually have critical weaknesses that have little or nothing to do with offices or with remote work.

Take [this article from Fortune](https://fortune.com/2023/05/05/openai-ceo-sam-altman-remote-work-mistake-return-to-office/) for example.

The article itself is not particularly interesting. It doesn't bother to do any analysis, and unquestioningly cites claims from various business leaders that no one bothers to support or refute with data. 

I won't bother responding to the headline claim from Sam Altman. It's mostly a factual question: does working remotely affect productivity, or does it not? I'm not a social scientist and can only answer from my own experience that _for me_ it does. Altman doesn't cite data at all, which isn't surprising since (at least based on the studies I've read) it doesn't support him very well.

I'm actually more interested in looking at the other claims in the piece. They represent a buffet of various qualitative arguments that are advanced in favor of return-to-office programs, and against the general idea of working remotely. And they're more or less representative of the kind of maxims that are often quoted in the press from business leaders.

Each of these arguments, though, can be turned on its head, and each reveals when so inverted that the argument actually has very little to do with working remotely or in the office. Instead, they highlight critical weaknesses in business process that can manifest in either working style, but which are often hidden from view in an in-office context.

---

Quoting VC Keith Rabois, we hear:

> Younger workers [...] “learn by osmosis” in a way that requires in-person interaction, and supervisors discover hidden talent by watching them.

I think there’s another way to frame this. What if I flipped the claim around, and said to you instead:

> Many companies struggle to train junior staff members effectively. Instead of preparing high quality onboarding operations, role-based documentation, and other critical learning aids, they rely on informal office co-location — which exposes their junior employees to the vagaries of whoever might be sitting next to them.

Neither of these claims actually has much to do with in-office or remote work. But when we think about the challenge from the perspective of a distributed organization, we find that an argument for in-office work is actually hiding a business weakness. 

Shouldn’t we train and onboard our employees in ways that are measureable, that are captureable, that are reportable? Why would we go backwards to a world where every onboarding is a one-off interaction, whose success or failure we cannot measure and which is driven as much by who sits next to whom as by rigorous understanding of the business?

The argument that's _nominally_ in favor of in-office work actually isn't. It's an argument for a clumsy, ad-hoc solution to onboarding (two people happening to be in the same physical location). That solution _might_ work well in some cases, but if it does, the fact that it works well is _accidental_ to the solution.

Next, we hear from James Clarke (a man who has recently been in the news for some repugnant conversations with his staff!) that he

> ... worries employees might be secretly working multiple jobs while working remotely. He also worries some might be freeing up time by using A.I. tools like OpenAI’s ChatGPT and GPT-4—time that his company isn’t utilizing.

There's an awful lot to unpack here. I could go on about Clarke's desperate and counterproductive grasping for control over his business. But what strikes me as most important here is that Clarke apparently has no idea how to measure the performance of his team, how to benchmark expected output from his staff, or even what he's paying his people for. (If they're salaried, it isn't the hours in their days).

Suppose I flip the script here, and instead tell you:

> Highly successful companies in 2023 measure workers based on output, not the number of hours worked. And, as AI technology rapidly matures, companies must have a vision for harnessing AI-driven productivity gains by freeing their workers from boilerplate tasks. The companies that win in 2024 will be those who find ways to use AI to unleash their employees’ creative time amd domain knowledge, delight their customers with deep relationship-building, and create more value than ever before.

In this version, I didn't actually say anything about remote work or in-office work.The argument has little to do with either as such. Clarke's argument, nominally in favor of in-office work, is actually trying to use office colocation to hide key business weaknesses. (It's also failing to grasp new opportunities to create value, expressing a rather pathetic possessiveness of employees' time, and generally creating the sense of a business leader as a petty tyrant). You don’t need to drive people back into the office so that they won’t use AI — you need to create contexts for them to use AI well, and in ways that multiply their impact on the bottom line!

But at a much more basic level, a leader that cannot effectively measure and evaluate their workers remotely _likely also cannot effectively measure and evaluate their workers in person_. And there's a good chance that those who claim to do so are not rigorously measuring productivity but are deliberately or otherwise applying other criteria, opening up risk that they're creating inequity in their businesses due to unconscious bias.

---

Lastly we hear from Arvind Krishna of IBM:

> [Krishna] also said while you might be just as productive working from home, “your career does suffer” if you work remotely.

Performance measurement again, but with a new wrinkle! 

Isn’t Krishna telling us here that IBM workers’ career ladders aren’t based on objective metrics, on productivity, on impact — but rather on social connections? That’s not something we should hold up as a model. Quite the opposite. We want to build a career ladder that rewards performance, vision, and impact, not schmoozing. We want to be able to show, with hard data, that we’re meeting our equity goals and promoting top performers.

Krishna's pointing to a common theme: to advance your career, you need "face time".

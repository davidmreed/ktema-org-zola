TL;DR: Promoting "minimalism" in software, and promoting specific software as "minimalist", hides underlying value judgements and silently privileges the judgements made by the speaker.

What does it mean for a piece of software to be "minimalist"? Of course, each exponent of this trope has their own sense, which may differ from one another. Usage suggests to me a few core tenets:

1. It uses less memory than alternatives.
2. It offers higher performance than alternatives (but is this shown?)
3. It has fewer features than alternatives, omitting features felt to be extraneous or focusing on delivering "what you need and not what you don't".

Points (1) and (2) are often but not always framed as consequences of (3).

> I'm not going to quote any exempla because I do not want to call anyone out. I suspect that most folks who spend a lot of time in the open source world will recognize this pattern.

---

Minimalism elevates personal preference to a virtue.

So-called minimalist software may indeed be excellent software (for some specific purpose, or in respect of specific quality characteristics). But it's not excellent because it's minimalist, because that word by itself means almost nothing. Minimalist really just connotes that the software focuses on a set of features or workflows felt by its creator to be necessary, or that it makes specific trade-offs of capability against size or speed.

_Articulate those tradeoffs instead of asserting minimalism_. This does at least three things for your discourse:

1. It makes your goals and intentions explicit. This helps reach audiences that share those specific trade-off decisions and definitions of essentiality, while avoiding impedance mismatches against those whose definition of what is minimal disagrees with your own.
2. It requires you to understand or acknowledge viewpoints other than your own. Why might a user who's very different from me not like this tool? We should not be hubristic about our software; nothing is the best for everyone.
3. It guides contributors to the software in working according to the actual principles that underlie its design.

---

I want to be clear that I am in no way speaking against building software that does just enough, that has a well-defined scope, that is fast and efficient, or that omits capabilities whose impact does not justify their expense.
